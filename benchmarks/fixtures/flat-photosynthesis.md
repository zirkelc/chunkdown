**Photosynthesis** is a [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_system) of [biological processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_process) by which [photopigment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photopigment)\-bearing [autotrophic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autotroph) [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism), such as most [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant), [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae) and [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria), convert [light energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_energy)—typically from [sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight)—into the [chemical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_energy) necessary to fuel their [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism). The term _photosynthesis_ usually refers to **oxygenic photosynthesis**, a process that releases [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) as a [byproduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byproduct) of [water splitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_splitting). Photosynthetic organisms store the converted chemical energy within the [bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond) of intracellular [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound) (complex [compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) containing [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon)), typically [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate) like [sugars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar) (mainly [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose), [fructose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructose) and [sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose)), [starches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch), [phytoglycogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytoglycogen) and [cellulose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose). When needing to use this stored energy, an organism's cells then metabolize the organic compounds through [cellular respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration). Photosynthesis plays a critical role in producing and maintaining the [oxygen content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_oxygen) of the [Earth's atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_atmosphere), and it supplies most of the [biological energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_energy) necessary for [complex life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_life) on [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth).

Some organisms also perform [anoxygenic photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoxygenic_photosynthesis), which does not produce oxygen. Some [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria) (e.g. [purple bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_bacteria)) use [bacteriochlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriochlorophyll) to split [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide) as a [reductant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductant) instead of [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water), releasing [sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur) instead of oxygen, which was a dominant form of photosynthesis in the [euxinic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euxinic) [Canfield oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canfield_ocean) during the [Boring Billion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boring_Billion). [Archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea) such as _[Halobacterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halobacterium)_ also perform a type of non-[carbon-fixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_fixation) anoxygenic photosynthesis, where the simpler photopigment [retinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retinal) and its [microbial rhodopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbial_rhodopsin) [derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_(chemistry)) are used to absorb green light and produce a proton ([hydron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydron)) [gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_gradient) across the [cell membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane), and the subsequent [ion movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiosmosis) powers [transmembrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmembrane_protein) [proton pumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_pump) to directly synthesize [adenosine triphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate) (ATP), the "energy currency" of cells. Such archaeal photosynthesis might have been the earliest form of photosynthesis that evolved on Earth, as far back as the [Paleoarchean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoarchean), preceding that of [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria) (see [Purple Earth hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_Earth_hypothesis)).

While the details may differ between [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species), the process always begins when light energy is absorbed by the [reaction centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_center), proteins that contain [photosynthetic pigments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetic_pigment) or [chromophores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromophore). In plants, these pigments are [chlorophylls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll) (a [porphyrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyrin) derivative that absorbs the red and blue [spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_spectrum) of light, thus reflecting green) held inside [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast), abundant in [leaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf) cells. In cyanobacteria, they are embedded in the [plasma membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_membrane). In these light-dependent reactions, some energy is used to strip [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) from suitable substances, such as water, producing oxygen gas. The [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) freed by the splitting of water is used in the creation of two important molecules that participate in energetic processes: reduced [nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinamide_adenine_dinucleotide_phosphate) (NADPH) and ATP.

In plants, [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae), and [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria), sugars are synthesized by a subsequent sequence of light-independent reactions called the [Calvin cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_cycle). In this process, atmospheric carbon dioxide is incorporated into already existing organic compounds, such as [ribulose bisphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribulose_bisphosphate) (RuBP). Using the ATP and NADPH produced by the light-dependent reactions, the resulting compounds are then [reduced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_reductant) and removed to form further carbohydrates, such as [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose). In other bacteria, different mechanisms like the [reverse Krebs cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_Krebs_cycle) are used to achieve the same end.

The first photosynthetic organisms probably [evolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved) early in the [evolutionary history of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_history_of_life) using [reducing agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducing_agent) such as hydrogen or hydrogen sulfide, rather than water, as sources of electrons. Cyanobacteria appeared later; the [excess oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_cycle) they produced contributed directly to the [oxygenation of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygenation_of_the_Earth), which rendered the evolution of complex life possible. The average rate of energy captured by global photosynthesis is approximately 130 [terawatts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terawatts), which is about eight times the total [power consumption of human civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_consumption_of_human_civilization). Photosynthetic organisms also convert around 100–115 billion [tons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ton) (91–104 Pg [petagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude_(mass)), or billions of metric tons), of carbon into [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_(ecology)) per year. Photosynthesis was discovered in 1779 by [Jan Ingenhousz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Ingenhousz) who showed that plants need light, not just soil and water.

Most photosynthetic organisms are [photoautotrophs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoautotroph), which means that they are able to [synthesize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_synthesis) food directly from [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) and [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) using [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) from light. However, not all organisms use carbon dioxide as a source of carbon atoms to carry out photosynthesis; [photoheterotrophs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoheterotroph) use organic compounds, rather than carbon dioxide, as a source of carbon.

In [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant), [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae), and [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria), photosynthesis releases oxygen. This **oxygenic photosynthesis** is by far the most common type of photosynthesis used by living organisms. Some shade-loving plants (sciophytes) produce such low levels of oxygen during photosynthesis that they use all of it themselves instead of releasing it to the atmosphere.

Although there are some differences between oxygenic photosynthesis in plants, algae, and cyanobacteria, the overall process is quite similar in these organisms. There are also many varieties of [anoxygenic photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoxygenic_photosynthesis), used mostly by bacteria, which consume carbon dioxide but do not release oxygen or which produce elemental sulfur instead of molecular oxygen.

Carbon dioxide is converted into sugars in a process called [carbon fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_fixation); photosynthesis captures energy from sunlight to convert carbon dioxide into [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate). Carbon fixation is an [endothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endothermic) [redox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) reaction. In general outline, photosynthesis is the opposite of [cellular respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration): while photosynthesis is a process of reduction of carbon dioxide to carbohydrates, cellular respiration is the oxidation of carbohydrates or other [nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient) to carbon dioxide. Nutrients used in cellular respiration include carbohydrates, amino acids and fatty acids. These nutrients are oxidized to produce carbon dioxide and water, and to release chemical energy to drive the organism's [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism).

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are distinct processes, as they take place through different sequences of chemical reactions and in different [cellular compartments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_compartment) (cellular respiration in [mitochondria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondria)).

Photosynthesis occurs in two stages. In the first stage, _light-dependent reactions_ or _light reactions_ capture the energy of light and use it to make the hydrogen carrier [NADPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NADPH) and the energy-storage molecule [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate). During the second stage, the _light-independent reactions_ use these products to capture and reduce carbon dioxide.

Most organisms that use oxygenic photosynthesis use [visible light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum) for the light-dependent reactions, although at least three use shortwave [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared) or, more specifically, far-red radiation.

Some organisms employ even more radical variants of photosynthesis. Some [archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea) use a simpler method that employs a pigment similar to those used for vision in animals. The [bacteriorhodopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriorhodopsin) changes its configuration in response to sunlight, acting as a [proton pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_pump). This produces a [proton gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_gradient) more directly, which is then converted to chemical energy. The process does not involve carbon dioxide fixation and does not release oxygen, and seems to have evolved separately from the more common types of photosynthesis.

In photosynthetic bacteria, the proteins that gather light for photosynthesis are embedded in [cell membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane). In its simplest form, this involves the membrane surrounding the cell itself. However, the membrane may be tightly folded into cylindrical sheets called [thylakoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylakoid), or bunched up into round [vesicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesicle_(biology)) called _intracytoplasmic membranes_. These structures can fill most of the interior of a cell, giving the membrane a very large surface area and therefore increasing the amount of light that the bacteria can absorb.

In plants and algae, photosynthesis takes place in [organelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organelle) called [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast). A typical [plant cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_cell) contains about 10 to 100 chloroplasts. The chloroplast is enclosed by a membrane. This membrane is composed of a phospholipid inner membrane, a phospholipid outer membrane, and an intermembrane space. Enclosed by the membrane is an aqueous fluid called the stroma. Embedded within the stroma are stacks of thylakoids (grana), which are the site of photosynthesis. The thylakoids appear as flattened disks. The thylakoid itself is enclosed by the thylakoid membrane, and within the enclosed volume is a lumen or thylakoid space. Embedded in the thylakoid membrane are integral and [peripheral membrane protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripheral_membrane_protein) complexes of the photosynthetic system.

Plants absorb light primarily using the [pigment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigment) [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll). The green part of the [light spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_spectrum) is not absorbed but is reflected, which is the reason that most plants have a green color. Besides chlorophyll, plants also use pigments such as [carotenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carotene) and [xanthophylls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanthophyll). Algae also use chlorophyll, but various other pigments are present, such as [phycocyanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phycocyanin), [carotenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carotene), and [xanthophylls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanthophyll) in [green algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_algae), [phycoerythrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phycoerythrin) in [red algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_algae) (rhodophytes) and [fucoxanthin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fucoxanthin) in [brown algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_algae) and [diatoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diatoms) resulting in a wide variety of colors.

These pigments are embedded in plants and algae in complexes called antenna proteins. In such proteins, the pigments are arranged to work together. Such a combination of proteins is also called a [light-harvesting complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-harvesting_complex).

Although all cells in the green parts of a plant have chloroplasts, the majority of those are found in specially adapted structures called [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf). Certain species adapted to conditions of strong sunlight and [aridity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid), such as many _[Euphorbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphorbia)_ and [cactus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cactus) species, have their main photosynthetic organs in their stems. The cells in the interior tissues of a leaf, called the [mesophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesophyll_tissue), can contain between 450,000 and 800,000 chloroplasts for every square millimeter of leaf. The surface of the leaf is coated with a water-resistant [waxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wax) [cuticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_cuticle) that protects the leaf from excessive [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation) of water and decreases the absorption of [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet) or [blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue) [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) to minimize [heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat). The transparent [epidermis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf#Epidermis) layer allows light to pass through to the [palisade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesophyll_tissue) mesophyll cells where most of the photosynthesis takes place.

In the [light-dependent reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-dependent_reactions), one [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) of the pigment [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll) absorbs one [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) and loses one [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron). This electron is taken up by a modified form of chlorophyll called [pheophytin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pheophytin), which passes the electron to a [quinone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinone) molecule, starting the flow of electrons down an [electron transport chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_transport_chain) that leads to the ultimate [reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) of [NADP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinamide_adenine_dinucleotide_phosphate) to [NADPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinamide_adenine_dinucleotide_phosphate). In addition, this creates a [proton gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_gradient) (energy gradient) across the [chloroplast membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast_membrane), which is used by [ATP synthase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATP_synthase) in the synthesis of [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate). The chlorophyll molecule ultimately regains the electron it lost when a [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) molecule is split in a process called [photolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photodissociation), which releases [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen).

The overall equation for the light-dependent reactions under the conditions of non-cyclic electron flow in green plants is:

2 H2O + 2 NADP + 3 ADP + 3 Pi + light → 2 NADPH + 2 H + 3 ATP + O2

Not all [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) of [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) can support photosynthesis. The photosynthetic [action spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_spectrum) depends on the type of [accessory pigments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accessory_pigment) present. For example, in [green plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_plants), the action spectrum resembles the [absorption spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_spectrum) for [chlorophylls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll) and [carotenoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carotenoid) with absorption peaks in violet-blue and red light. In [red algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_algae), the action spectrum is blue-green light, which allows these [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae) to use the blue end of the spectrum to grow in the deeper waters that filter out the longer wavelengths (red light) used by above-ground green plants. The non-absorbed part of the [light spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum) is what gives [photosynthetic organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetic_organism) their [color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color) (e.g., green plants, red algae, [purple bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_bacteria)) and is the least effective for photosynthesis in the respective [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism).

In [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant), [light-dependent reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-dependent_reaction) occur in the [thylakoid membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylakoid_membrane) of the [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast) where they drive the synthesis of [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate) and [NADPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NADPH). The light-dependent reactions are of two forms: [cyclic and non-cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photophosphorylation).

In the non-cyclic reaction, the photons are captured in the light-harvesting [antenna complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_complex) of [photosystem II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosystem) by [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll) and other [accessory pigments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accessory_pigments) (see diagram "Z-scheme"). The absorption of a photon by the antenna complex loosens an electron by a process called [photoinduced charge separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoinduced_charge_separation). The antenna system is at the core of the [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll) molecule of the photosystem II reaction center. That loosened electron is taken up by the primary [electron-acceptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_acceptor) molecule, [pheophytin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pheophytin). As the electrons are shuttled through an [electron transport chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_transfer_chain) (the so-called _Z-scheme_ shown in the diagram), a [chemiosmotic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiosmotic_potential) is generated by pumping [proton cations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydron_(chemistry)) ([H](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen)) across the [membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane) and into the [thylakoid space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylakoid_space). An ATP synthase [enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme) uses that [chemiosmotic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiosmotic_potential) to make ATP during [photophosphorylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photophosphorylation), whereas [NADPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NADPH) is a product of the terminal [redox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) reaction in the _Z-scheme_. The electron enters a chlorophyll [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) in [Photosystem I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosystem_I). There it is further excited by the [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) absorbed by that [photosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosystem). The electron is then passed along a chain of [electron acceptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_acceptor) to which it transfers some of its [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy). The energy delivered to the electron acceptors is used to move [hydrogen ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_ion) across the thylakoid membrane into the [lumen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumen_(anatomy)). The electron is eventually used to [reduce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) the coenzyme [NADP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinamide_adenine_dinucleotide_phosphate) with an [H](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydron_(chemistry)) to NADPH (which has functions in the light-independent reaction); at that point, the path of that electron ends.

The cyclic reaction is similar to that of the non-cyclic but differs in that it generates only ATP, and no reduced NADP (NADPH) is created. The cyclic reaction takes place only at photosystem I. Once the electron is displaced from the photosystem, the electron is passed down the electron acceptor molecules and returns to photosystem I, from where it was emitted, hence the name _cyclic reaction_.

[Linear electron transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis#Z_scheme) through a photosystem will leave the [reaction center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetic_reaction_centre) of that photosystem [oxidized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidized). Elevating another electron will first require re-reduction of the reaction center. The excited electrons lost from the reaction center ([P700](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P700)) of [photosystem I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosystem_I) are replaced by transfer from [plastocyanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastocyanin), whose electrons come from electron transport through [photosystem II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosystem_II). Photosystem II, as the first step of the _Z-scheme_, requires an external source of electrons to reduce its oxidized [chlorophyll _a_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll_a) reaction center. The source of electrons for photosynthesis in green plants and [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria) is water. Two water molecules are oxidized by the energy of four successive charge-separation reactions of photosystem II to yield a molecule of [diatomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diatomic_molecule) oxygen and four [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) ions. The electrons yielded are transferred to a redox-active [tyrosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrosine) residue that is oxidized by the energy of [P680](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P680). This resets the ability of P680 to absorb another photon and release another [photo-dissociated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photodissociation) electron. The oxidation of water is [catalyzed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis) in photosystem II by a redox-active structure that contains four [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese) ions and a [calcium ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_ion); this [oxygen-evolving complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen-evolving_complex) binds two [water molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_molecules) and contains the four oxidizing equivalents that are used to drive the water-oxidizing reaction (Kok's S-state diagrams). The hydrogen ions are released in the [thylakoid lumen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylakoid#Lumen) and therefore contribute to the transmembrane chemiosmotic potential that leads to [ATP synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATP_synthesis). Oxygen is a [waste product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-product) of light-dependent reactions, but the majority of organisms on [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth) use oxygen and its energy for [cellular respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration), including [photosynthetic organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetic_organism).

In the [light-independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-independent_reactions) (or "dark") reactions, the enzyme [RuBisCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RuBisCO) captures [CO2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) from the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_atmosphere) and, in a [process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_process) called the [Calvin cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_cycle), uses the newly formed [NADPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NADPH) and releases [three-carbon sugars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triose), which are later [combined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensation_reaction) to form [sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose) and [starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch). The overall equation for the light-independent reactions in [green plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_plants) is

3 CO2 + 9 ATP + 6 NADPH + 6 H → C3H6O3\-phosphate + 9 ADP + 8 Pi + 6 NADP + 3 H2O

[Carbon fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_fixation) produces the [three-carbon sugar intermediate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyceraldehyde_3-phosphate), which is then converted into the final [carbohydrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate) products. The [simple carbon sugars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monosaccharide) photosynthesis produces are then used to form other [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound), such as the building material [cellulose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose), the [precursors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precursor_(chemistry)) for [lipid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipid) and [amino acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid) biosynthesis, or as a fuel in [cellular respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration). The latter occurs not only in [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant) but also in [animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal) when the [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) and [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) from plants is passed through a [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_chain).

The [fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_carbon_fixation) or [reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) of [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) is a process in which carbon dioxide combines with a [five-carbon sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five-carbon_sugar), [ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribulose_1,5-bisphosphate), to [yield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_(chemistry)) two [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) of a three-carbon compound, [glycerate 3-phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycerate_3-phosphate), also known as 3-phosphoglycerate. Glycerate 3-phosphate, in the presence of [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate) and [NADPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NADPH) produced during the light-dependent stages, is reduced to [glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyceraldehyde_3-phosphate). This [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_(chemistry)) is also referred to as 3-phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL) or, more generically, as [triose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triose) phosphate. Most (five out of six molecules) of the glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate produced are used to regenerate ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate so the process can continue. The triose phosphates not thus "recycled" often condense to form [hexose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexose) phosphates, which ultimately yield [sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose), [starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch), and [cellulose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose), as well as [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose) and [fructose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructose). The [sugars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar) produced during carbon [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism) yield [carbon skeletons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_skeleton) that can be used for other [metabolic reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic_reaction) like the production of [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acids) and [lipids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipids).

In [hot and dry conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerophyte), plants close their [stomata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomata) to prevent water loss. Under these conditions, CO2 will decrease and oxygen [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas), produced by the [light reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_reactions) of photosynthesis, will increase, causing an increase of [photorespiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photorespiration) by the [oxygenase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygenase) activity of [ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubisco) (RuBisCO) and decrease in carbon fixation. Some plants have [evolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution) mechanisms to increase the CO2 concentration in the leaves under these conditions.

Plants that use the [C4 carbon fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C4_carbon_fixation) process chemically fix carbon dioxide in the [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_(biology)) of the [mesophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesophyll) by adding it to the three-carbon molecule [phosphoenolpyruvate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoenolpyruvate) (PEP), a reaction [catalyzed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis) by an [enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme) called [PEP carboxylase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoenolpyruvate_carboxylase), creating the four-carbon organic acid [oxaloacetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxaloacetic_acid). Oxaloacetic acid or [malate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malate) synthesized by this process is then [translocated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translocation_(botany)) to specialized [bundle sheath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundle_sheath) cells where the enzyme [RuBisCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RuBisCO) and other Calvin cycle enzymes are located, and where CO2 released by [decarboxylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decarboxylation) of the four-carbon acids is then fixed by RuBisCO activity to the three-carbon [3-phosphoglyceric acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-phosphoglyceric_acid). The physical separation of RuBisCO from the oxygen-generating light reactions reduces photorespiration and increases CO2 fixation and, thus, the [photosynthetic capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetic_capacity) of the [leaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf). [C4 plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C4_carbon_fixation) can produce more sugar than [C3 plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C3_carbon_fixation) in conditions of high light and [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermophile). Many important [crop plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_plants) are C4 plants, including [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize), [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum), [sugarcane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane), and [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet). Plants that do not use PEP-carboxylase in carbon fixation are called C3 plants because the primary [carboxylation reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxylation), catalyzed by RuBisCO, produces the three-carbon 3-phosphoglyceric acids directly in the [Calvin-Benson cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin-Benson_cycle). Over 90% of plants use C3 carbon fixation, compared to 3% that use C4 carbon fixation; however, the evolution of C4 in over sixty plant lineages makes it a striking example of [convergent evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_evolution). [C2 photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C2_photosynthesis), which involves carbon-concentration by selective breakdown of photorespiratory glycine, is both an evolutionary precursor to C4 and a useful [carbon-concentrating mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis#Carbon_concentrating_mechanisms) in its own right.

[Xerophytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerophytes), such as [cacti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cacti) and most [succulents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Succulents), also use PEP carboxylase to capture carbon dioxide in a process called [Crassulacean acid metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAM_photosynthesis) (CAM). In contrast to C4 metabolism, which _spatially_ separates the CO2 fixation to PEP from the Calvin cycle, CAM _temporally_ separates these two processes. CAM plants have a different [leaf anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf#Anatomy) from C3 plants, and fix the CO2 at night, when their stomata are open. CAM plants store the CO2 mostly in the form of [malic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malic_acid) via carboxylation of [phosphoenolpyruvate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoenolpyruvate) to [oxaloacetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxaloacetate), which is then reduced to malate. Decarboxylation of malate during the day releases CO2 inside the leaves, thus allowing carbon fixation to 3-phosphoglycerate by RuBisCO. CAM is used by 16,000 [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) of plants.

[Calcium-oxalate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_oxalate)\-accumulating plants, such as _[Amaranthus hybridus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaranthus_hybridus)_ and _[Colobanthus quitensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colobanthus_quitensis),_ show a variation of photosynthesis where calcium oxalate [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druse_(botany)) function as dynamic [carbon pools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sink), supplying carbon dioxide (CO2) to photosynthetic cells when stomata are partially or totally closed. This process was named [alarm photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alarm_photosynthesis). Under [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_(biology)) conditions (e.g., [water deficit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_scarcity)), [oxalate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxalate) released from calcium oxalate crystals is converted to CO2 by an [oxalate oxidase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxalate_oxidase) enzyme, and the produced CO2 can support the [Calvin cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_cycle) reactions. Reactive [hydrogen peroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_peroxide) (H2O2), the [byproduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-product) of oxalate oxidase reaction, can be [neutralized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutralization_(chemistry)) by [catalase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalase). Alarm photosynthesis represents a photosynthetic variant to be added to the well-known C4 and CAM pathways. However, alarm photosynthesis, in contrast to these pathways, operates as a biochemical pump that collects carbon from the organ interior (or from the [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) and not from the atmosphere.

[Cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria) possess [carboxysomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxysome), which increase the concentration of CO2 around RuBisCO to increase the rate of photosynthesis. An enzyme, [carbonic anhydrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonic_anhydrase), located within the carboxysome, releases CO2 from dissolved [hydrocarbonate ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbonate) (HCO
3). Before the CO2 can diffuse out, RuBisCO concentrated within the carboxysome quickly sponges it up. HCO
3 ions are made from CO2 outside the cell by another carbonic anhydrase and are actively pumped into the cell by a membrane protein. They cannot cross the membrane as they are charged, and within the cytosol they turn back into CO2 very slowly without the help of carbonic anhydrase. This causes the HCO
3 ions to accumulate within the cell from where they diffuse into the carboxysomes. [Pyrenoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrenoid) in [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae) and [hornworts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hornwort) also act to concentrate CO2 around RuBisCO.

The overall [process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_process) of photosynthesis takes place in four stages:

| Stage | Event | Site | Time scale |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | [Energy transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transfer) in [antenna chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-harvesting_complex) | [Thylakoid membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylakoid_membranes) in the [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast) | [Femtosecond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femtosecond) to [picosecond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picosecond) |
| 2 | [Transfer of electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_transfer) in [photochemical reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photochemical_reactions) | [Picosecond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picosecond) to [nanosecond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanosecond) |   |
| 3 | [Electron transport chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_transport_chain) and [ATP synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATP_synthesis) | [Microsecond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsecond) to [millisecond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millisecond) |   |
| 4 | [Carbon fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_carbon_fixation) and export of stable [products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_(chemistry)) | [Stroma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroma_(fluid)) of the chloroplasts and the cell [cytosol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytosol) | [Millisecond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millisecond) to [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second) |

[Plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant) usually [convert light into chemical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photophosphorylation) with a [photosynthetic efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetic_efficiency) of 3–6%. Absorbed light that is unconverted is [dissipated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissipation) primarily as [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat), with a small [fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction) (1–2%) reemitted as [chlorophyll fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll_fluorescence) at longer (redder) [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength). This fact allows [measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement) of the [light reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_reactions) of photosynthesis by using chlorophyll [fluorometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorometer).

Actual plants' photosynthetic efficiency varies with the [frequency of the light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency#Light) being converted, [light intensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irradiance), [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature), and proportion of [carbon dioxide in the atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_in_Earth's_atmosphere), and can vary from 0.1% to 8%. By comparison, [solar panels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photovoltaic_module) convert light into [electric energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_energy) at an efficiency of approximately 6–20% for [mass-produced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_production) panels, and above 40% in [laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory) devices. [Scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientist) are studying photosynthesis in hopes of developing plants with increased [yield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_yield).

The efficiency of both light and dark reactions can be measured, but the relationship between the two can be complex. For example, the [light reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_reactions) creates [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate) and [NADPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NADPH) energy [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule), which [C
3 plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C3_plants) can use for [carbon fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_carbon_fixation) or [photorespiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photorespiration). [Electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) may also flow to other electron sinks. For this reason, it is not uncommon for [authors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Author) to differentiate between work done under [non-photorespiratory conditions and under photorespiratory conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photorespiration#Conditions_which_affect_photorespiration).

[Chlorophyll fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll_fluorescence) of [photosystem II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosystem_II) can measure the light reaction, and [infrared gas analyzers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_gas_analyzer) can measure the [dark reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_reaction). An integrated chlorophyll [fluorometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorometer) and [gas exchange system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas-exchange_system) can investigate both light and dark reactions when researchers use the two separate [systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Systems) together. Infrared gas analyzers and some [moisture sensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hygrometer) are sensitive enough to measure the [photosynthetic assimilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assimilation_(biology)) of [CO
2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CO2) and of [Δ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_change)H
2O using [reliable methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodology). CO
2 is commonly measured in μmols/([m](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_metre)/[s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second)), [parts per](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts-per_notation) million, or volume per million; and [H
2O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H2O) is commonly measured in mmols/(m/s) or in mbars. By measuring [CO
2 assimilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CO2_assimilation), ΔH
2O, leaf temperature, [barometric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barometric_pressure), leaf area, and [photosynthetically active radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetically_active_radiation) (PAR), it becomes possible to estimate, "A" or carbon assimilation, "E" or [transpiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transpiration), "gs" or [stomatal conductance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomatal_conductance), and "Ci" or intracellular CO
2. However, it is more common to use chlorophyll fluorescence for [plant stress measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_stress_measurement), where appropriate, because the most commonly used parameters [FV/FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_stress_measurement#Fv/Fm) and [Y(II) or F/FM'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_stress_measurement#Y(II)_or_ΔF/Fm'_and_ETR) can be measured in a few seconds, allowing the investigation of larger plant populations.

[Gas exchange systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas-exchange_system) that offer control of CO
2 levels, above and below [ambient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_pressure), allow the common practice of measurement of A/Ci curves, at different CO
2 levels, to characterize a plant's photosynthetic response.

Integrated chlorophyll fluorometer – gas exchange systems allow a more [precise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accuracy_and_precision) measure of photosynthetic response and mechanisms. While standard gas exchange photosynthesis systems can measure Ci, or substomatal CO
2 levels, the addition of integrated chlorophyll fluorescence measurements allows a more precise measurement of CC, the estimation of CO
2 concentration at the site of [carboxylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxylation) in the chloroplast, to replace Ci. CO
2 concentration in the chloroplast becomes possible to estimate with the measurement of mesophyll conductance or gm using an integrated system.

Photosynthesis measurement systems are not designed to directly measure the amount of light the leaf absorbs, but analysis of [chlorophyll fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll_fluorescence), [P700](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P700)\- and P515-absorbance, and [gas exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_exchange) measurements reveal detailed information about, e.g., the [photosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosystem), [quantum efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_efficiency) and the CO
2 assimilation rates. With some instruments, even wavelength dependency of the photosynthetic efficiency can be [analyzed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis).

A [phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon) known as [quantum walk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_walk) increases the efficiency of the energy transport of light significantly. In the photosynthetic cell of an [alga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae), [bacterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria), or plant, there are light-sensitive molecules called [chromophores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromophore) arranged in an antenna-shaped structure called a photocomplex. When a [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) is absorbed by a chromophore, it is converted into a [quasiparticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasiparticle) referred to as an [exciton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exciton), which jumps from chromophore to chromophore towards the reaction center of the photocomplex, a collection of molecules that traps its energy in a chemical form accessible to the cell's metabolism. The exciton's wave properties enable it to cover a wider area and try out several possible paths simultaneously, allowing it to instantaneously "choose" the most efficient route, where it will have the highest probability of arriving at its destination in the minimum possible time.

Because that quantum walking takes place at temperatures far higher than quantum phenomena usually occur, it is only possible over very short distances. Obstacles in the form of destructive interference cause the particle to lose its wave properties for an instant before it regains them once again after it is freed from its locked position through a classic "hop". The movement of the electron towards the photo center is therefore covered in a series of conventional hops and quantum walks.

[Fossils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil) of what are thought to be [filamentous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_filament) photosynthetic [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism) have been dated at 3.4 billion years old. More recent [studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research) also suggest that photosynthesis may have begun about 3.4 billion years ago, though the first direct [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence) of photosynthesis comes from [thylakoid membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylakoid_membranes) preserved in 1.75-billion-year-old [cherts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chert).

[Oxygenic photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_evolution) is the main source of [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) in the [Earth's atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth), and its earliest appearance is sometimes referred to as the [oxygen catastrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_catastrophe). [Geological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology) evidence suggests that oxygenic photosynthesis, such as that in [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria), became important during the [Paleoproterozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoproterozoic) era around two billion years ago. Modern photosynthesis in [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant) and most photosynthetic [prokaryotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prokaryote) is oxygenic, using [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) as an [electron donor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_donor), which is [oxidized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidized) to molecular oxygen in the [photosynthetic reaction center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetic_reaction_center).

Several groups of [animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal) have formed [symbiotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiosis) relationships with photosynthetic [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae). These are most common in [corals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral), [sponges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge), and [sea anemones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_anemone). [Scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientist) presume that this is due to the particularly simple [body plans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_plan) and large [surface areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_area) of these animals compared to their [volumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume). In addition, a few marine [mollusks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusks), such as _[Elysia viridis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elysia_viridis)_ and _[Elysia chlorotica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elysia_chlorotica),_ also maintain a symbiotic relationship with [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast) they capture from the algae in [their diet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusca#Eating,_digestion,_and_excretion) and then store in their bodies (see [Kleptoplasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleptoplasty)). This allows the mollusks to survive solely by photosynthesis for several months at a time. Some of the [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene) from the plant [cell nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_nucleus) have even been transferred to the [slugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slug), so that the chloroplasts can be supplied with [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) they need to survive.

An even closer form of symbiosis may explain the origin of chloroplasts. Chloroplasts have many similarities with photosynthetic [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria), including a circular [chromosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosome), prokaryotic-type [ribosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribosome), and similar [proteins in the photosynthetic reaction center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetic_reaction_centre_protein_family). The [endosymbiotic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endosymbiotic_theory) suggests that photosynthetic bacteria were acquired (by [endocytosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocytosis)) by early [eukaryotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eukaryotic) cells to form the first plant cells. Therefore, chloroplasts may be photosynthetic bacteria that adapted to life inside plant cells. Like [mitochondria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondria), chloroplasts possess their own [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), separate from the [nuclear DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_DNA) of their plant host cells and the genes in this chloroplast DNA resemble those found in [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria). DNA in chloroplasts codes for [redox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) proteins such as those found in the photosynthetic reaction centers. The [CoRR Hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CoRR_Hypothesis) proposes that this co-location of genes with their gene products is required for redox regulation of [gene expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_expression), and accounts for the persistence of DNA in bioenergetic [organelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organelle).

Symbiotic and [kleptoplastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleptoplasty) organisms excluded:

-   The [glaucophytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaucophyte) and the [red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_algae) and [green algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viridiplantae)—clade [Archaeplastida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeplastida) ([uni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicellular_organism)\- and [multicellular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multicellular_organism))
-   The [cryptophytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptophyceae)—clade [Cryptista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptista) (unicellular)
-   The [haptophytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haptophyte)—clade [Haptista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haptista) (unicellular)
-   The [dinoflagellates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinoflagellate) and [chromerids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromerida) in the superphylum [Myzozoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myzozoa), and [Pseudoblepharisma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoblepharisma) in the phylum [Ciliophora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciliate)—clade [Alveolata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alveolata) (unicellular)
-   The [ochrophytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochrophyta)—clade [Stramenopila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stramenopile) (uni- and multicellular)
-   The [chlorarachniophytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorarachniophyte) and three [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) of [Paulinella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinella) in the phylum [Cercozoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cercozoa)—clade [Rhizaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhizaria) (unicellular)
-   The [euglenids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euglenid)—clade [Excavata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excavata) (unicellular)

Except for the euglenids, which are found within the [Excavata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excavata), all of these belong to the [Diaphoretickes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaphoretickes). Archaeplastida and the photosynthetic Paulinella got their plastids, which are surrounded by two membranes, through primary [endosymbiosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endosymbiont) in two separate events, by engulfing a cyanobacterium. The plastids in all the other groups have either a red or green algal origin, and are referred to as the "red lineages" and the "green lineages". The only known exception is the ciliate [Pseudoblepharisma tenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoblepharisma), which in addition to its plastids that originated from green algae also has a [purple sulfur bacterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_sulfur_bacteria) as symbiont. In dinoflagellates and euglenids the plastids are surrounded by three membranes, and in the remaining lines by four. A [nucleomorph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleomorph), remnants of the original algal nucleus located between the inner and outer membranes of the plastid, is present in the cryptophytes (from a red alga) and chlorarachniophytes (from a green alga). Some dinoflagellates that lost their photosynthetic ability later regained it again through new endosymbiotic events with different algae. While able to perform photosynthesis, many of these eukaryotic groups are [mixotrophs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixotroph) and practice [heterotrophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterotroph) to various degrees.

Early photosynthetic systems, such as those in [green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_sulfur_bacteria) and [purple sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_sulfur) and [green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroflexota) and [purple nonsulfur bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_nonsulfur_bacteria), are thought to have been [anoxygenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoxygenic_photosynthesis), and used various other molecules than water as [electron donors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_donor). Green and purple sulfur bacteria are thought to have used [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) and [sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur) as electron donors. Green nonsulfur bacteria used various [amino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid) and other [organic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_acid) as electron donors. Purple nonsulfur bacteria used a variety of nonspecific organic molecules. The use of these molecules is consistent with the geological evidence that Earth's early atmosphere was highly [reducing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducing_environment) at [that time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Earth#Hadean_and_Archean_Eons).

With a possible exception of [Heimdallarchaeota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asgard_(archaea)), photosynthesis is not found in [archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea). [Haloarchaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haloarchaea) are [photoheterotrophic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoheterotroph); they can absorb energy from the sun, but do not harvest carbon from the atmosphere and are therefore not photosynthetic. Instead of chlorophyll they use rhodopsins, which convert light-energy to ion gradients but cannot mediate electron transfer reactions.

In [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria) eight photosynthetic lineages are currently known:

-   [Cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria), the only prokaryotes performing oxygenic photosynthesis and the only prokaryotes that contain two types of photosystems (type I (RCI), also known as Fe-S type, and type II (RCII), also known as quinone type). The seven remaining prokaryotes have [anoxygenic photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoxygenic_photosynthesis) and use versions of either type I or type II.
-   [Chlorobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_sulfur_bacteria) (green sulfur bacteria) Type I
-   [Heliobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliobacteria) Type I
-   [Chloracidobacterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloracidobacterium) Type I
-   [Proteobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudomonadota) (purple sulfur bacteria and purple non-sulfur bacteria) Type II (see: [Purple bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_bacteria))
-   [Chloroflexota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroflexota) (green non-sulfur bacteria) Type II
-   [Gemmatimonadota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemmatimonadota) Type II
-   Eremiobacterota Type II

The biochemical capacity to use water as the source for electrons in photosynthesis evolved once, in a [common ancestor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_ancestor) of extant [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria) (formerly called blue-green algae). The geological record indicates that this transforming event took place early in Earth's history, at least 2450–2320 million years ago (Ma), and, it is speculated, much earlier. Because the Earth's atmosphere contained almost no oxygen during the estimated development of photosynthesis, it is believed that the first photosynthetic cyanobacteria did not generate oxygen. Available evidence from geobiological studies of [Archean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archean) (>2500 Ma) [sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock) indicates that life existed 3500 Ma, but the question of when oxygenic photosynthesis evolved is still unanswered. A clear paleontological window on cyanobacterial [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution) opened about 2000 Ma, revealing an already-diverse biota of cyanobacteria. Cyanobacteria remained the principal [primary producers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_producers) of oxygen throughout the [Proterozoic Eon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proterozoic_Eon) (2500–543 Ma), in part because the redox structure of the oceans favored photoautotrophs capable of [nitrogen fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_fixation). [Green algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_algae) joined cyanobacteria as the major primary producers of oxygen on [continental shelves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelves) near the end of the [Proterozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proterozoic), but only with the [Mesozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesozoic) (251–66 Ma) radiations of dinoflagellates, coccolithophorids, and diatoms did the [primary production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_production) of oxygen in marine shelf waters take modern form. Cyanobacteria remain critical to [marine ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_ecosystem) as [primary producers of oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_cycle) in oceanic gyres, as agents of biological nitrogen fixation, and, in modified form, as the [plastids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastid) of marine algae.

Although some of the steps in photosynthesis are still not completely understood, the overall photosynthetic equation has been known since the 19th century.

[Jan van Helmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Baptist_van_Helmont) began the [research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research) of the [process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_process) in the mid-17th century when he carefully measured the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) of the [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) a [plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant) was using and the mass of the plant as it grew. After noticing that the soil mass changed very little, he [hypothesized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis) that the mass of the [growing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_development) plant must come from the [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water), the only [substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance) he added to the potted plant. His hypothesis was partially [accurate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accuracy_and_precision) – much of the gained mass comes from [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) as well as water. However, this was a signaling point to the idea that the bulk of a plant's [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_(ecology)) comes from the inputs of photosynthesis, not the soil itself.

[Joseph Priestley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Priestley), a [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist) and [minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_(Christianity)), discovered that when he isolated a [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume) of air under an inverted [jar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jar) and burned a [candle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candle) in it (which gave off [CO2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide)), the candle would burn out very quickly, much before it ran out of [wax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candle#Wax). He further discovered that a [mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouse) could similarly ["injure"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breathing) air. He then showed that a plant could restore the air the candle and the mouse had "injured".

In 1779, [Jan Ingenhousz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Ingenhousz) repeated Priestley's [experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experiment). He discovered that it was the influence of [sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight) on the plant that could cause it to revive a mouse in a matter of hours.

In 1796, [Jean Senebier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Senebier), a Swiss [pastor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastor), [botanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botanist), and [naturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalist), [demonstrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_demonstration) that [green plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_plants) consume carbon dioxide and release oxygen under the influence of [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light). Soon afterward, [Nicolas-Théodore de Saussure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas-Théodore_de_Saussure) showed that the increase in mass of the plant as it grows could not be due only to uptake of CO2 but also to the incorporation of water. Thus, the basic [reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction) by which [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism) use photosynthesis to produce [food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food) (such as [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose)) was outlined.

[Cornelis Van Niel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelis_Van_Niel) made key discoveries explaining the [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) of photosynthesis. By studying [purple sulfur bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_sulfur_bacteria) and [green bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_bacteria), he was the first to demonstrate that photosynthesis is a light-dependent [redox reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) in which hydrogen [reduces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducing_agent) (donates its [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) as [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) and [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) to) carbon dioxide.

[Robert Emerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Emerson_(scientist)) discovered two light reactions by testing plant productivity using different wavelengths of light. With the red alone, the light reactions were suppressed. When blue and red were combined, the output was much more substantial. Thus, there were two photosystems, one absorbing up to 600 nm wavelengths, the other up to 700 nm. The former is known as PSII, the latter is PSI. PSI contains only chlorophyll "a", PSII contains primarily chlorophyll "a" with most of the available chlorophyll "b", among other pigments. These include phycobilins, which are the red and blue pigments of red and blue algae, respectively, and fucoxanthol for brown algae and diatoms. The process is most productive when the absorption of quanta is equal in both PSII and PSI, assuring that input energy from the antenna complex is divided between the PSI and PSII systems, which in turn powers the photochemistry.

[Robert Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Hill_(biochemist)) thought that a complex of reactions consisted of an intermediate to cytochrome b6 (now a plastoquinone), and that another was from cytochrome f to a step in the carbohydrate-generating mechanisms. These are linked by plastoquinone, which does require energy to reduce cytochrome f. Further experiments to prove that the oxygen developed during the photosynthesis of green plants came from water were performed by Hill in 1937 and 1939. He showed that isolated [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast) give off oxygen in the presence of unnatural reducing agents like [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) [oxalate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxalate), [ferricyanide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferricyanide) or [benzoquinone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzoquinone) after exposure to light. In the Hill reaction:

2 H2O + 2 A + (light, chloroplasts) → 2 AH2 + O2

A is the electron acceptor. Therefore, in light, the electron acceptor is reduced and oxygen is evolved. [Samuel Ruben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Ruben) and [Martin Kamen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Kamen) used [radioactive isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radionuclide) to determine that the oxygen liberated in photosynthesis came from the water.

[Melvin Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Calvin) and [Andrew Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Benson), along with [James Bassham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Bassham), elucidated the path of carbon assimilation (the photosynthetic carbon reduction cycle) in plants. The carbon reduction cycle is known as the [Calvin cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_cycle), but many scientists refer to it as the Calvin-Benson, Benson-Calvin, or even Calvin-Benson-Bassham (or CBB) Cycle.

[Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize)–winning scientist [Rudolph A. Marcus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_A._Marcus) was later able to discover the function and significance of the electron transport chain.

[Otto Heinrich Warburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Heinrich_Warburg) and [Dean Burk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_Burk) discovered the I-quantum photosynthesis reaction that splits CO2, activated by the respiration.

In 1950, first experimental evidence for the existence of [photophosphorylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photophosphorylation) _in vivo_ was presented by [Otto Kandler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Kandler) using intact _[Chlorella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorella)_ cells and interpreting his findings as light-dependent [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate) formation. In 1954, [Daniel I. Arnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_I._Arnon) et al. discovered photophosphorylation _in vitro_ in isolated [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast) with the help of P.

Louis N. M. Duysens and Jan Amesz discovered that chlorophyll "a" will absorb one light, oxidize cytochrome f, while chlorophyll "a" (and other pigments) will absorb another light but will reduce this same oxidized cytochrome, stating the two light reactions are in series.

In 1893, the American botanist [Charles Reid Barnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Reid_Barnes) proposed two terms, _photosyntax_ and _photosynthesis_, for the biological process of _synthesis of complex carbon compounds out of carbonic acid, in the presence of chlorophyll, under the influence of light_. The term _photosynthesis_ is derived from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) _phōs_ ([φῶς](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/φῶς#Ancient_Greek), 'gleam') and _sýnthesis_ ([σύνθεσις](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/σύνθεσις#Ancient_Greek), 'arranging together'), while _photosyntax_ comes from _sýntaxis_ ([σύνταξις](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/σύνταξις#Ancient_Greek), 'configuration'). Over time, the term _photosynthesis_ came into common usage. Later discovery of anoxygenic photosynthetic bacteria and photophosphorylation necessitated redefinition of the term.

In the late 1940s at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), the details of photosynthetic carbon metabolism were sorted out by the chemists [Melvin Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Calvin), Andrew Benson, James Bassham and a score of students and researchers utilizing the carbon-14 isotope and paper chromatography techniques. The pathway of CO2 fixation by the algae _Chlorella_ in a fraction of a second in light resulted in a three carbon molecule called phosphoglyceric acid (PGA). For that original and ground-breaking work, a [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry) was awarded to Melvin Calvin in 1961. In parallel, plant physiologists studied leaf gas exchanges using the new method of infrared gas analysis and a leaf chamber where the net photosynthetic rates ranged from 10 to 13 μmol CO2·m·s, with the conclusion that all terrestrial plants have the same photosynthetic capacities, that are light saturated at less than 50% of sunlight.

Later in 1958–1963 at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University), field grown [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) was reported to have much greater leaf photosynthetic rates of 40 μmol CO2·m·s and not be saturated at near full sunlight. This higher rate in maize was almost double of those observed in other species such as wheat and soybean, indicating that large differences in photosynthesis exist among higher plants. At the University of Arizona, detailed gas exchange research on more than 15 species of [monocots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocotyledon) and [dicots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicotyledon) uncovered for the first time that differences in leaf anatomy are crucial factors in differentiating photosynthetic capacities among species. In tropical grasses, including maize, sorghum, sugarcane, Bermuda grass and in the dicot amaranthus, leaf photosynthetic rates were around 38−40 μmol CO2·m·s, and the leaves have two types of green cells, i.e. outer layer of mesophyll cells surrounding a tightly packed cholorophyllous vascular bundle sheath cells. This type of anatomy was termed Kranz anatomy in the 19th century by the botanist [Gottlieb Haberlandt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottlieb_Haberlandt) while studying leaf anatomy of sugarcane. Plant species with the greatest photosynthetic rates and Kranz anatomy showed no apparent photorespiration, very low CO2 compensation point, high optimum temperature, high stomatal resistances and lower mesophyll resistances for gas diffusion and rates never saturated at full sun light. The research at Arizona was designated a Citation Classic in 1986. These species were later termed C4 plants as the first stable compound of CO2 fixation in light has four carbons as malate and aspartate. Other species that lack Kranz anatomy were termed C3 type such as cotton and sunflower, as the first stable carbon compound is the three-carbon PGA. At 1000 ppm CO2 in measuring air, both the C3 and C4 plants had similar leaf photosynthetic rates around 60 μmol CO2·m·s indicating the suppression of photorespiration in C3 plants.

There are four main factors influencing photosynthesis and several corollary factors. The four main are:

-   Light [irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irradiance) and [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength)
-   Water absorption
-   [Carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration)
-   [Temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature).

Total photosynthesis is limited by a range of environmental factors. These include the amount of light available, the amount of [leaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf) area a plant has to capture light (shading by other plants is a major limitation of photosynthesis), the rate at which carbon dioxide can be supplied to the [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast) to support photosynthesis, the availability of water, and the availability of suitable temperatures for carrying out photosynthesis.

The process of photosynthesis provides the main input of free energy into the biosphere, and is one of four main ways in which radiation is important for plant life.

The radiation climate within plant communities is extremely variable, in both time and space.

In the early 20th century, [Frederick Blackman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Blackman) and [Gabrielle Matthaei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabrielle_Matthaei) investigated the effects of light intensity ([irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irradiance)) and temperature on the rate of carbon assimilation.

-   At constant temperature, the rate of carbon assimilation varies with irradiance, increasing as the irradiance increases, but reaching a plateau at higher irradiance.
-   At low irradiance, increasing the temperature has little influence on the rate of carbon assimilation. At constant high irradiance, the rate of carbon assimilation increases as the temperature is increased.

These two experiments illustrate several important points: First, it is known that, in general, [photochemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photochemical) reactions are not affected by [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature). However, these experiments clearly show that temperature affects the rate of carbon assimilation, so there must be two sets of reactions in the full process of carbon assimilation. These are the light-dependent 'photochemical' temperature-independent stage, and the light-independent, temperature-dependent stage. Second, Blackman's experiments illustrate the concept of [limiting factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_factor). Another limiting factor is the wavelength of light. Cyanobacteria, which reside several meters underwater, cannot receive the correct wavelengths required to cause photoinduced charge separation in conventional photosynthetic pigments. To combat this problem, Cyanobacteria have a light-harvesting complex called [Phycobilisome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phycobilisome). This complex is made up of a series of proteins with different pigments which surround the reaction center.

As carbon dioxide concentrations rise, the rate at which sugars are made by the light-independent reactions increases until limited by other factors. [RuBisCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RuBisCO), the enzyme that captures carbon dioxide in the light-independent reactions, has a binding affinity for both carbon dioxide and oxygen. When the concentration of carbon dioxide is high, RuBisCO will fix carbon dioxide. However, if the carbon dioxide concentration is low, RuBisCO will bind oxygen instead of carbon dioxide. This process, called [photorespiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photorespiration), uses energy, but does not produce sugars.

RuBisCO oxygenase activity is disadvantageous to plants for several reasons:

1.  One product of oxygenase activity is phosphoglycolate (2 carbon) instead of [3-phosphoglycerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-phosphoglycerate) (3 carbon). Phosphoglycolate cannot be metabolized by the Calvin-Benson cycle and represents carbon lost from the cycle. A high oxygenase activity, therefore, drains the sugars that are required to recycle ribulose 5-bisphosphate and for the continuation of the [Calvin-Benson cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin-Benson_cycle).
2.  Phosphoglycolate is quickly metabolized to glycolate that is toxic to a plant at a high concentration; it inhibits photosynthesis.
3.  Salvaging glycolate is an energetically expensive process that uses the glycolate pathway, and only 75% of the carbon is returned to the Calvin-Benson cycle as 3-phosphoglycerate. The reactions also produce [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia) (NH3), which is able to [diffuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_diffusion) out of the plant, leading to a loss of nitrogen.

A highly simplified summary is:

2 glycolate + ATP → 3-phosphoglycerate + carbon dioxide + ADP + NH3

The salvaging pathway for the products of RuBisCO oxygenase activity is more commonly known as photorespiration, since it is characterized by light-dependent oxygen consumption and the release of carbon dioxide.
