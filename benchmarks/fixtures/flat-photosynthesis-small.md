**Photosynthesis** is a [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_system) of [biological processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_process) by which [photopigment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photopigment)\-bearing [autotrophic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autotroph) [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism), such as most [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant), [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae) and [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria), convert [light energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_energy)—typically from [sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight)—into the [chemical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_energy) necessary to fuel their [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism). The term _photosynthesis_ usually refers to **oxygenic photosynthesis**, a process that releases [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) as a [byproduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byproduct) of [water splitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_splitting). Photosynthetic organisms store the converted chemical energy within the [bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond) of intracellular [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound) (complex [compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) containing [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon)), typically [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate) like [sugars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar) (mainly [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose), [fructose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructose) and [sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose)), [starches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch), [phytoglycogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytoglycogen) and [cellulose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose). When needing to use this stored energy, an organism's cells then metabolize the organic compounds through [cellular respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration). Photosynthesis plays a critical role in producing and maintaining the [oxygen content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_oxygen) of the [Earth's atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_atmosphere), and it supplies most of the [biological energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_energy) necessary for [complex life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_life) on [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth).

Some organisms also perform [anoxygenic photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoxygenic_photosynthesis), which does not produce oxygen. Some [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria) (e.g. [purple bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_bacteria)) use [bacteriochlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriochlorophyll) to split [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide) as a [reductant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductant) instead of [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water), releasing [sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur) instead of oxygen, which was a dominant form of photosynthesis in the [euxinic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euxinic) [Canfield oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canfield_ocean) during the [Boring Billion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boring_Billion). [Archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea) such as _[Halobacterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halobacterium)_ also perform a type of non-[carbon-fixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_fixation) anoxygenic photosynthesis, where the simpler photopigment [retinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retinal) and its [microbial rhodopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbial_rhodopsin) [derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_(chemistry)) are used to absorb green light and produce a proton ([hydron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydron)) [gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_gradient) across the [cell membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane), and the subsequent [ion movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiosmosis) powers [transmembrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmembrane_protein) [proton pumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_pump) to directly synthesize [adenosine triphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate) (ATP), the "energy currency" of cells. Such archaeal photosynthesis might have been the earliest form of photosynthesis that evolved on Earth, as far back as the [Paleoarchean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoarchean), preceding that of [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria) (see [Purple Earth hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_Earth_hypothesis)).

While the details may differ between [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species), the process always begins when light energy is absorbed by the [reaction centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_center), proteins that contain [photosynthetic pigments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthetic_pigment) or [chromophores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromophore). In plants, these pigments are [chlorophylls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll) (a [porphyrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyrin) derivative that absorbs the red and blue [spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_spectrum) of light, thus reflecting green) held inside [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast), abundant in [leaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf) cells. In cyanobacteria, they are embedded in the [plasma membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_membrane). In these light-dependent reactions, some energy is used to strip [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) from suitable substances, such as water, producing oxygen gas. The [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) freed by the splitting of water is used in the creation of two important molecules that participate in energetic processes: reduced [nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinamide_adenine_dinucleotide_phosphate) (NADPH) and ATP.

In plants, [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae), and [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria), sugars are synthesized by a subsequent sequence of light-independent reactions called the [Calvin cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_cycle). In this process, atmospheric carbon dioxide is incorporated into already existing organic compounds, such as [ribulose bisphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribulose_bisphosphate) (RuBP). Using the ATP and NADPH produced by the light-dependent reactions, the resulting compounds are then [reduced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_reductant) and removed to form further carbohydrates, such as [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose). In other bacteria, different mechanisms like the [reverse Krebs cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_Krebs_cycle) are used to achieve the same end.

The first photosynthetic organisms probably [evolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved) early in the [evolutionary history of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_history_of_life) using [reducing agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducing_agent) such as hydrogen or hydrogen sulfide, rather than water, as sources of electrons. Cyanobacteria appeared later; the [excess oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_cycle) they produced contributed directly to the [oxygenation of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygenation_of_the_Earth), which rendered the evolution of complex life possible. The average rate of energy captured by global photosynthesis is approximately 130 [terawatts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terawatts), which is about eight times the total [power consumption of human civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_consumption_of_human_civilization). Photosynthetic organisms also convert around 100–115 billion [tons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ton) (91–104 Pg [petagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude_(mass)), or billions of metric tons), of carbon into [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_(ecology)) per year. Photosynthesis was discovered in 1779 by [Jan Ingenhousz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Ingenhousz) who showed that plants need light, not just soil and water.

Most photosynthetic organisms are [photoautotrophs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoautotroph), which means that they are able to [synthesize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_synthesis) food directly from [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) and [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) using [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) from light. However, not all organisms use carbon dioxide as a source of carbon atoms to carry out photosynthesis; [photoheterotrophs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoheterotroph) use organic compounds, rather than carbon dioxide, as a source of carbon.

In [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant), [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae), and [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria), photosynthesis releases oxygen. This **oxygenic photosynthesis** is by far the most common type of photosynthesis used by living organisms. Some shade-loving plants (sciophytes) produce such low levels of oxygen during photosynthesis that they use all of it themselves instead of releasing it to the atmosphere.

Although there are some differences between oxygenic photosynthesis in plants, algae, and cyanobacteria, the overall process is quite similar in these organisms. There are also many varieties of [anoxygenic photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoxygenic_photosynthesis), used mostly by bacteria, which consume carbon dioxide but do not release oxygen or which produce elemental sulfur instead of molecular oxygen.

Carbon dioxide is converted into sugars in a process called [carbon fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_fixation); photosynthesis captures energy from sunlight to convert carbon dioxide into [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate). Carbon fixation is an [endothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endothermic) [redox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) reaction. In general outline, photosynthesis is the opposite of [cellular respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration): while photosynthesis is a process of reduction of carbon dioxide to carbohydrates, cellular respiration is the oxidation of carbohydrates or other [nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient) to carbon dioxide. Nutrients used in cellular respiration include carbohydrates, amino acids and fatty acids. These nutrients are oxidized to produce carbon dioxide and water, and to release chemical energy to drive the organism's [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism).

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are distinct processes, as they take place through different sequences of chemical reactions and in different [cellular compartments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_compartment) (cellular respiration in [mitochondria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondria)).

Photosynthesis occurs in two stages. In the first stage, _light-dependent reactions_ or _light reactions_ capture the energy of light and use it to make the hydrogen carrier [NADPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NADPH) and the energy-storage molecule [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate). During the second stage, the _light-independent reactions_ use these products to capture and reduce carbon dioxide.

Most organisms that use oxygenic photosynthesis use [visible light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum) for the light-dependent reactions, although at least three use shortwave [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared) or, more specifically, far-red radiation.

Some organisms employ even more radical variants of photosynthesis. Some [archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea) use a simpler method that employs a pigment similar to those used for vision in animals. The [bacteriorhodopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriorhodopsin) changes its configuration in response to sunlight, acting as a [proton pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_pump). This produces a [proton gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_gradient) more directly, which is then converted to chemical energy. The process does not involve carbon dioxide fixation and does not release oxygen, and seems to have evolved separately from the more common types of photosynthesis.

In photosynthetic bacteria, the proteins that gather light for photosynthesis are embedded in [cell membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane). In its simplest form, this involves the membrane surrounding the cell itself. However, the membrane may be tightly folded into cylindrical sheets called [thylakoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylakoid), or bunched up into round [vesicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesicle_(biology)) called _intracytoplasmic membranes_. These structures can fill most of the interior of a cell, giving the membrane a very large surface area and therefore increasing the amount of light that the bacteria can absorb.

In plants and algae, photosynthesis takes place in [organelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organelle) called [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast). A typical [plant cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_cell) contains about 10 to 100 chloroplasts. The chloroplast is enclosed by a membrane. This membrane is composed of a phospholipid inner membrane, a phospholipid outer membrane, and an intermembrane space. Enclosed by the membrane is an aqueous fluid called the stroma. Embedded within the stroma are stacks of thylakoids (grana), which are the site of photosynthesis. The thylakoids appear as flattened disks. The thylakoid itself is enclosed by the thylakoid membrane, and within the enclosed volume is a lumen or thylakoid space. Embedded in the thylakoid membrane are integral and [peripheral membrane protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripheral_membrane_protein) complexes of the photosynthetic system.

Plants absorb light primarily using the [pigment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigment) [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll). The green part of the [light spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_spectrum) is not absorbed but is reflected, which is the reason that most plants have a green color. Besides chlorophyll, plants also use pigments such as [carotenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carotene) and [xanthophylls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanthophyll). Algae also use chlorophyll, but various other pigments are present, such as [phycocyanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phycocyanin), [carotenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carotene), and [xanthophylls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanthophyll) in [green algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_algae), [phycoerythrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phycoerythrin) in [red algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_algae) (rhodophytes) and [fucoxanthin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fucoxanthin) in [brown algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_algae) and [diatoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diatoms) resulting in a wide variety of colors.

These pigments are embedded in plants and algae in complexes called antenna proteins. In such proteins, the pigments are arranged to work together. Such a combination of proteins is also called a [light-harvesting complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-harvesting_complex).

Although all cells in the green parts of a plant have chloroplasts, the majority of those are found in specially adapted structures called [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf). Certain species adapted to conditions of strong sunlight and [aridity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid), such as many _[Euphorbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphorbia)_ and [cactus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cactus) species, have their main photosynthetic organs in their stems. The cells in the interior tissues of a leaf, called the [mesophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesophyll_tissue), can contain between 450,000 and 800,000 chloroplasts for every square millimeter of leaf. The surface of the leaf is coated with a water-resistant [waxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wax) [cuticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_cuticle) that protects the leaf from excessive [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation) of water and decreases the absorption of [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet) or [blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue) [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) to minimize [heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat). The transparent [epidermis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf#Epidermis) layer allows light to pass through to the [palisade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesophyll_tissue) mesophyll cells where most of the photosynthesis takes place.

In the [light-dependent reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-dependent_reactions), one [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) of the pigment [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll) absorbs one [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) and loses one [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron). This electron is taken up by a modified form of chlorophyll called [pheophytin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pheophytin), which passes the electron to a [quinone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinone) molecule, starting the flow of electrons down an [electron transport chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_transport_chain) that leads to the ultimate [reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) of [NADP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinamide_adenine_dinucleotide_phosphate) to [NADPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinamide_adenine_dinucleotide_phosphate). In addition, this creates a [proton gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_gradient) (energy gradient) across the [chloroplast membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast_membrane), which is used by [ATP synthase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATP_synthase) in the synthesis of [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate).
